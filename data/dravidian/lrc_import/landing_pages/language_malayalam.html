Malayalam (മലയാളം, [mɐlɐjaːɭɐm]) is a Dravidian language spoken primarily in the Indian state of Kerala and the union territories of Lakshadweep and Puducherry, where it is an official language. It is also spoken by linguistic minorities in the Kodagu and Dakshina Kannada districts of Karnataka, and the Kanyakumari, Coimbatore, and Nilgiris districts of Tamil Nadu. Malayalam-speaking diaspora communities also exist in North America, the Persian Gulf, Europe, and Australia.

Malayalam is designated as a classical language by the government of India. While its origins are debated, it is generally considered the descendant of the West Coast dialect of medieval Tamil that gradually developed into a distinct language. Ethnologue (2026) approximates 37 million L1 speakers of Malayalam around the world, with a combined L1 and L2 total of 38 million speakers.

Writing System

Written Malayalam utilizes an abugida script that evolved from the Brahmi script. Written from left to right syllabically, it has 15 vowels, 36 to 41 basic consonants, and six voiceless consonants.

The earliest script used to write Malayalam was the Vatteluttu script, which came from the Tamil-Brahmi scripts. The modern Malayalam script is an extension of the Vatteluttu alphabet and the Grantha script, which is another Brahmi script used to write Sanskrit texts.

Notable Malayalam texts

The earliest known Malayalam texts include Ramacaritram (12th or 13th century) and Thirunizhalmala (13th century). An additional notable classical work is Bhashakautalyam (12th century, based on Chanakya’s Arthashastra), discussing statecraft and military strategy. Contemporary Malayalam literature and poetry often discuss sociopolitical issues; the first major novel in Malayalam, Indulekha (1889), features themes of caste, feudalism, and women’s empowerment.
